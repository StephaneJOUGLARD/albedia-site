Cool roofs: principle, benefits and limits

A cool roof is a roof made highly reflective and highly emissive, most often with a light-coloured coating applied to the existing roof covering. Here is how it works, what the measurements say and when it is really useful.

The principle: send the sun back instead of storing it

When the sun strikes a roof, part of the radiation is reflected and the rest is absorbed, then turned into heat. This heat passes through the roof and spreads into the building, all the faster when the roof is poorly insulated.

A cool roof coating works in two ways. It is highly reflective: it sends most of the radiation back to the sky. It is also highly emissive: the heat it does capture is quickly released as infrared radiation, instead of building up in the roof.

Albedo in practice

Albedo, or solar reflectance, is the share of solar radiation that a surface reflects. It ranges from 0, when everything is absorbed, to 1, when everything is reflected. Whatever is not reflected is absorbed.

Change the albedo of the roof to see the share of radiation it absorbs, and where common roofs stand.

Reflectance, emissivity, SRI: three values to know

They appear on the technical data sheets of coatings and in the conditions of incentive schemes.

Solar reflectance (albedo)
Ratio between the solar radiation reflected and the radiation received, from 0 to 1. The higher it is, the less the surface heats up in the sun.
Thermal emissivity
Ability of a surface to release its heat as infrared radiation, from 0 to 1. Cool roof coatings generally have an emissivity of 0.80 to 0.90 or more.
Solar reflectance index (SRI)
The SRI combines both values according to the ASTM E1980 standard. It is 0 for a reference black and 100 for a reference white. The best coatings exceed 100.

What the measurements say

Orders of magnitude from published work, mainly in the United States, where cool roofs have been studied for a long time.

  • 5 to 15%The share of solar radiation reflected by a traditional roof. It therefore absorbs 85 to 95% of the energy received. US EPA
  • 66 to 85 °CThe temperature a traditional roof can reach in high summer. A reflective roof stays about 28 to 33 °C cooler at the hottest time of day. US EPA
  • 6 to 11 °CThe usual difference between a reflective roof and the ambient air, compared with 31 to 47 °C for a conventional roof. US EPA
  • 30 °CThe difference measured on a summer afternoon between a black roof and a white roof. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory
  • 2 to 40%Air-conditioning savings found by a review of more than 25 studies, 20% on average, with peak demand reductions of 14 to 38%. US EPA
  • 1.2 to 3.3 °CReduction in peak indoor temperature observed in homes without air conditioning. US EPA

The same coating gives very different results depending on the building. Among the cases reviewed by the US EPA, a pharmacy in San José, whose roof already let little heat through, saved only 2% on its air conditioning: when little heat enters through the roof, there is little to gain.

When is a cool roof relevant?

A study carried out for the French Building Federation compared several buildings and French climates. Its conclusions are clear: the value depends first of all on how the building is used.

{{icon:check}}Favourable balance

  • Buildings where air conditioning accounts for more than 30% of the needs, like many shops and industrial sites
  • Air-conditioned, unheated buildings
  • Buildings in the south of France

{{icon:loupe}}Worth studying

  • Non-air-conditioned buildings that overheat in summer, like many warehouses and workshops
  • Roofs with little or no insulation
  • Temperate climates with increasingly hot summers

{{icon:fermer}}Little value or to be avoided

  • Mainly heated buildings: when heating accounts for 75% of the needs, the effect is barely significant
  • Roofs where water ponds, for reflective paints
  • Surfaces that are too damaged and must be redone before any coating

What about winter?

A reflective roof also reflects the winter sun, which can slightly increase heating needs. According to the US EPA, in most of the climates studied, this penalty does not cancel out the summer savings.

It weighs more in cold regions, for buildings that are heavily heated. This is why we assess the summer and winter balance before proposing a cool roof, in particular at altitude or in northern regions.

{{icon:thermo}}In summer

High sun, long days: the roof receives most of the year’s solar gains.

{{icon:clim}}In winter

Low sun, short days, often overcast skies: gains through the roof are small.

Lifespan and maintenance

Properly applied, many cool roof coatings have lasted more than twenty years. A poorly prepared application, on the other hand, can peel or flake within a few years: surface preparation matters as much as the product.

Reflectance decreases mainly in the first year, due to soiling, by about 20% with proper maintenance. Smooth, sloped surfaces get less dirty. Cleaning in line with the manufacturer’s recommendations helps to restore performance.

The French Building Federation recommends handing the client a maintenance sheet at the end of the works and offering an annual maintenance contract. That is what we do.

Beyond the building: urban heat islands

In some American cities, roofs and roads make up more than 60% of surfaces. Dark and dry, they absorb the sun and warm the air: this is the urban heat island effect, which makes heatwaves worse and increases air-conditioning needs.

Lighter-coloured roofs contribute less to it. A British study cited by the US EPA estimates that, on the scale of a city, reflective roofs could offset 18% of heat-island-related mortality.
